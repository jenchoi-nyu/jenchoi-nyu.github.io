Week 1 — Proposal: Shaping Story Through Sound

Elevator Pitch

Echo + Form explores how technology can transform pottery into a deeply personal, interactive experience. By integrating personal data—such as memories, emotions, and gestures—into the creation of ceramic vessels, this project turns objects into storytelling artifacts that hold emotional weight over time. It merges craft, technology, and emotional reflection to create meaningful, interactive objects that resonate on both a personal and collective level.

Key Elements

  • How emotions and memories shape objects’ meaning
  • The role of interactive technology in craft and storytelling
  • Pottery as a medium for personal and cultural identity

Audience

  • People interested in personal storytelling and emotional expression
  • Artists, designers, and technologists exploring new forms of interactive craft
  • Educators and researchers in design, material culture, and digital fabrication

Research Conducted

  • Emotional Objects & Museums: Observations from the 9/11 Memorial Museum, exploring how artifacts carry both personal and collective significance
  • Affect & Cultural Theory: Engaged with Sara Ahmed’s affect theory and Stuart Hall’s encoding/decoding framework to understand how meaning is assigned to material objects
  • Pottery & Personalization: Studies on how intention, personalization, and tactile engagement in ceramics enhance emotional connection

What I’m Making (WIP)

  • Interactive Clay Vessels: Using personal inputs (voice, heartbeat, gestures) to shape digital vessel forms
  • 3D-Printed Slip-Casting Molds: Enabling broader accessibility in personalized ceramic creation
  • Digital Models: Designing a real-time interface for users to generate their own vessels through sound-driven algorithms

Why I’m Making This

The motivation behind Echo + Form lies in exploring how everyday objects can become emotional artifacts—vessels not just in form, but in function, capable of holding memory, identity, and presence. This project bridges traditional craft and emerging technologies, revealing how deeply personal data can be embedded in a physical material like clay. Ultimately, it asks: what does it mean to give shape to feeling, and how might that form live with us?

The project aims to explore how emotions, personal data, and interactive technology can shape ceramic vessels, transforming them into meaningful storytelling artifacts. With this goal in mind, I created a timeline, mapped deliverables, and began defining the relationship between voice, technology, and form.

This planning phase helped me think beyond individual components—voice, clay, tech—and focus on the holistic experience. From interaction flow to exhibition format, each step is designed to guide the user in crafting a vessel that feels like a piece of themselves.

Week 3 — On Feedback, Vulnerability, and Finding Form

Resources

  • Conversation: Critique by Eva Sutton, from The Art of Critical Making (2013)
  • “Find the Coaching in Criticism” by Sheila Heen and Douglas Stone (Harvard Business Review, 2014)
  • Thanks for the Feedback | Doug Stone & Sheila Heen | Talks at Google

Reflections

This week, I confronted the dual nature of feedback—its ability to both clarify and destabilize. I shared my concept with peers and mentors: a system where voice, music, and personal story shape a ceramic vessel. The idea felt abstract to some and deeply resonant to others. The mixed reactions taught me that presenting a hybrid craft-tech-emotion project requires a clear explanation of both process and purpose.

I asked for feedback on several fronts: Does it make sense that a voice could shape form? Are the input methods (motion, heartbeat, questionnaire) too varied or just right? Does this actually communicate emotion—or just represent it? I wanted to know whether people felt connected to the idea, or distanced by the tech. The responses helped illuminate where the project succeeds (personal resonance, materiality, storytelling) and where it needs clarity (interaction flow, material feasibility, technical precision).

The Type of Information I’m Looking For

  • Concept Clarity: Does the narrative of emotion-to-object transformation come through?
  • User Interaction: Are input methods intuitive and compelling?
  • Material & Technical Feasibility: Does the hybrid of digital design and clay 3D printing align with craft tradition?
  • Emotional Impact: Does this feel like more than just a digital fabrication experiment?

My Past Relationship with Feedback

I’ve always valued specific, actionable feedback. I thrive when critiques point out what works and why, what’s unclear and how to rethink it. I struggle when responses are too vague (“It’s cool!”) or contradictory. To navigate this, I’ve learned to ask better questions—direct, specific, and intentional. This week was a reminder that design is not just making, it’s conversing: with your audience, your materials, and your own intuition.

Takeaway

Vulnerability is part of creative growth. Feedback invited me to listen more closely—not only to others, but to what the project is becoming. This week, Echo + Form began to solidify not just in structure, but in spirit. I’m learning to trust the shape that’s emerging through iteration, conversation, and reflection.

Week 5: Progress Presentation ↗

Progress Reflection

This week marked my first formal presentation of Echo + Form since refining the conceptual and technical framework. Standing before peers and faculty, I introduced the core idea: translating a person’s voice and music into a vessel that visually and tactilely reflects who they are. It was a moment of clarity—and exposure. I shared early prototypes, digital mockups, and a walk-through of the system, from voice recording to parametric modeling to clay output.

The feedback was valuable: many appreciated the emotional resonance and multisensory engagement. Some asked about the scalability of the fabrication process or accessibility of the voice-to-form interaction. One major takeaway: I need to clarify the interface and data-to-form logic for non-designers without losing poetic depth.

After the Show: The Gallery of Characters

Reflections

Developing these personas helped me refine my messaging and understand the project’s potential impact. They each hold a mirror to different aspects of the work—artistic, skeptical, scientific. Moving forward, I’ll use them to test how well I’m communicating the emotional and technical dimensions of Echo + Form to a diverse audience.

I’m beginning to see the vessel not just as a metaphor, but as a container for questions, resistance, memory, and resonance.

Week 6 — Writing the Vessel: Annotated Paper Outline

Overview

This week I shifted from clay and code to words—writing an annotated outline of my thesis paper. The process helped clarify the conceptual arc of Echo + Form, not just as a design project, but as a cultural investigation into how voice, memory, and material interact. The outline frames the project’s key questions, theoretical foundations, and design methods, while laying the groundwork for how I will discuss emotional resonance, user experience, and material experimentation.

Annotated Structure

1. Introduction: This section situates the project by describing its aim: translating the personal (voice, music) into a tangible object. I establish the project’s focus on interactivity, craft, and emotional design, and explain how pottery and sound serve as metaphors and materials for memory.

2. Research & Theoretical Foundations: Drawing on Sara Ahmed’s affect theory and Stuart Hall’s encoding/decoding model, this chapter explores how emotions are shared and socially shaped. I also reference Roman Ondák and Jennifer Rochlin to show how artists have explored memory and personal imprint in material form.

3. Design Methodology: This section explains the tools and logic behind the voice-to-vessel process. I describe how vocal pitch, cadence, and volume are mapped to form, texture, and proportion through parametric modeling, and how user interaction (e.g., saying “This belongs to [Name]”) serves as a trigger for customization.

4. Prototyping & Interaction: Here, I walk through the development of the physical and digital prototypes, from voice capture to clay printing. I describe experiments with 3D-printed porcelain, challenges in translating audio data, and the iterative process of refining form through both user feedback and material constraints.

5. Testing & Refinement: This chapter outlines my plan for user testing: recruiting participants, capturing their stories, and translating their voices into vessels. I’ll analyze emotional resonance, usability, and how the final objects are received. Feedback loops are core here—how the vessel reflects its origin and how users respond to their physicalized voice.

6. Final Outcomes: A showcase of the final vessels, the design evolution, and a reflection on their emotional and cultural significance. I’ll also discuss the exhibition format and how it invites broader engagement through interaction and story collection.

7. Appendices: Documentation of prototypes, user data, design diagrams, and references.

Reflections

Writing the outline helped me solidify Echo + Form’s interdisciplinary foundation. It’s more than a design experiment; it’s an inquiry into the emotional life of objects and the role of voice as a cultural and personal imprint. I now feel more confident structuring my thesis around the emotional arc of transformation—from sound to form to story.

Week 7 — First Forms from Voice

Prototyping Progress

This week marked a turning point: I created the first physical vessels shaped directly by the human voice. After weeks of refining the sound-to-form logic and sketching out concepts, I shifted from visual prototypes to tangible output. Using parametric modeling, I began translating real-time voice data—specifically, people saying their names—into three-dimensional ceramic forms. My process evolved significantly from earlier explorations. I initially experimented with a continuous spiral, where a single line rose in space with radius changes dictated by sound. While it visually echoed how a 3D printer layers clay, the result didn’t feel meaningful enough—spoken names are brief, and the spiral method favored longer, more complex sounds. Instead, I pivoted to a more intentional structure. The final approach added a new ring to the vessel every few frames, using pitch, volume, and rhythm to subtly distort each layer’s shape. These rings were stacked upward to form a smooth, spiraling silhouette—an aesthetic that aligned more closely with traditional wheel-thrown pottery and additive 3D printing alike.

User Reactions

I invited a few participants to record their names using the real-time demo built in p5.js. Watching their vessel take shape instantly sparked curiosity and reflection. One person described the result as “seeing your voice wear a body.” Another asked whether more emotional expressions—like whispering or crying—could also be captured in clay. These moments emphasized the emotional depth embedded in the project.

Reflections

This week reminded me that voice is more than sound—it’s breath, memory, identity. Each form carries the imprint of a person’s presence in that exact moment. Even with the same name, every voice produces a unique outcome because no two utterances are alike. Technically, I still need to fine-tune extrusion parameters—especially pressure stability and drying rates—but conceptually, this week confirmed something important: this system offers a new language for expressing identity. Next, I’ll explore how to translate not just voice, but memory and music, onto the vessel’s surface.

Week 8 — Stories in Clay: User Testing Insights

Voice Becomes Vessel

This week, I conducted my first full round of user testing for Echo + Form. Though my original vision involved 3D clay printing, a mechanical breakdown shifted my process back to traditional wheel-throwing. Each participant recorded the phrase “This belongs to [Name],” and I used their vocal qualities—pitch, volume, cadence—as interpretive input to hand-throw unique ceramic vessels.

Higher-pitched voices led to taller, more slender forms. Softer voices produced delicate profiles. Rhythmic delivery influenced contour variations. Instead of automating this translation, I became the medium—interpreting sound with my hands on the wheel. The process was slow but rewarding, reinforcing the emotional potential of handmade translation.

Emotion in Interpretation

The most compelling outcome was participants’ surprise at how personally their voice was captured. One user whispered her name and received a gentle, narrow pot; another's sharp, percussive rhythm shaped bold ridges. A participant with a stutter reflected, “It feels like my voice has value—it’s been interpreted, not corrected.” That moment affirmed the goal of this project: honoring the emotional nuances embedded in everyday speech.

Material as Memory Keeper

Working without digital automation deepened my connection to each piece. The act of throwing with a voice in mind became a kind of listening. I found that clay, when treated as a responsive medium rather than a passive substrate, can hold complexity, variation, and care. The absence of GCODE became a strength—human touch offered a different kind of precision.

Reflection

This week reinforced the power of craft to mediate technology. By interpreting vocal data manually, I moved beyond replication into resonance. These voice-formed vessels are not just representations—they’re conversations made tactile. As I move into surface treatment next, I’m thinking about how music might add yet another emotional register to these clay diaries.

Expanding the Input: From Voice to Music

After translating voice into the structure of the vessel, I turned my attention to the surface: how could personal music—specifically, a favorite song—be embedded in the clay in a way that communicates emotional resonance? This week, I began developing a manual “music-to-pattern” process based on listening, interpretation, and tactile carving.

Participants submitted a meaningful song along with a short memory or story tied to it. Rather than use software to generate patterns, I carefully listened to each song—mapping elements like rhythm, tempo, dynamics, and silence into hand-carved surface textures. Up-tempo songs yielded rapid notches and jagged textures; slower ballads resulted in sweeping, continuous lines.

Surface as Story

These markings were inscribed directly onto the leather-hard clay vessels, wrapping around the form like tactile timelines. A participant’s nostalgic piano piece became a vessel etched with concentric arcs and flowing grooves. Another’s glitchy punk track produced erratic marks like visual noise. These surfaces added a second emotional layer to the form—where voice embodied presence, texture now held memory.

One user said, “I can feel my song,” while tracing their fingers across the clay. That tactile intimacy—memory made touchable—became the heart of this week’s experiment.

Translation Without Code

Without digital fabrication, the biggest challenge was consistency across scale. What felt intuitive by hand had to remain legible once fired. I tested different tools, from loop carvers to pin tools, to find patterns that were durable, expressive, and haptically engaging. This analog approach emphasized interpretation over precision—each vessel became a one-off emotional portrait.

Reflection

This week extended the project’s emotional vocabulary. Voice gave the vessels structure; music gave them surface and story. Together, these two modalities began to feel like a duet—an inner and outer world made of clay. As I prepare to stage the vessels in a collective installation, I’m thinking about what it means for many voices and memories to coexist in physical space.

Week 10 — A Constellation of Vessels: Building a Collective Portrait + Paper Due

From Individual to Collective

This week marked a transition from designing individual vessels to composing them together as a collective constellation. As each vessel holds a personal story—its form shaped by voice, its surface by music—I started exploring how these artifacts can be arranged to reflect shared experience. Can emotional objects, when placed in relation, become a map of affective diversity? What might it mean to witness other people’s stories embodied in form and clay?

I experimented with layout designs for the final exhibition: radial arrangements (like sound waves rippling out), linear timelines (progressing from low to high frequencies). Each approach offered different metaphors—ripples of connection, gradients of memory, or sonic constellations.

Paper Due: Writing as Assembly

In parallel, I submitted the near-final draft of my thesis paper. This process helped crystallize the project's conceptual throughlines—especially the shift from individual emotion to collective resonance. As I wrote about Ahmed’s affect theory and Hall’s encoding/decoding, I realized how central this interplay is to the project: the objects don’t just hold memory—they transmit it. They are receivers and broadcasters.

The most challenging section was articulating how surface pattern (derived from music) functions as both aesthetic and archive. I drew on examples like Roman Ondák’s Measuring the Universe to explain how accumulated data in space becomes a social artifact. The vessels, too, are data—personal but legible to others.

Testing Spatial Experience

I ran a brief user walkthrough of a mock display: 6 vessels on plinths, surrounded by QR codes linking to audio clips and backstories. Observers commented on the “echoing” effect—how voice and music intersected physically and digitally. One participant suggested allowing visitors to “compose” their own constellation by rearranging vessels on-screen, offering an interactive extension to the physical installation.

That insight inspired me to prototype a touch-screen element that allows users to drag vessels across a digital grid, remixing emotional data into shared topographies. A speculative but exciting direction for the final week.

Looking Ahead

With the paper submitted and vessels nearing completion, the next phase is presentation design: how to stage these objects so their stories are felt and not just seen. I’ll also finalize audio integration—embedding recordings of names and songs into an interactive kiosk.

The constellation is taking shape. What began as individual sonic impressions is becoming a shared landscape of presence.

Week 11 — Clay Meets Code: The Output Experimentation

From Digital Form to Physical Artifact

Prompt: How do digital interpretations of emotional input meet the physical limitations of traditional materials?

This Week’s Focus: Bridging the digital design logic with the handmade ceramic process—mapping voice-based parameters into vessel profiles, and finalizing how music data would influence surface texture.

Design Challenge

This week marked a turning point in the project: translating digital input—particularly the voice data—into tangible, handmade form. Since I shifted from 3D printing to traditional wheel-throwing due to technical constraints, this transition required both conceptual and technical reworking. How does one convert a waveform into a vessel without an automated fabrication method? The solution lay in using the voice data as a guide—almost like a score or notation system—rather than a direct output.

Mapping Voice to Form

Working in Rhino/Grasshopper, I continued refining the sound-to-form parameters. The pitch still influenced the width of the belly of the vessel; cadence helped suggest rhythm and changes in wall shape; volume corresponded to height. I translated these variables into hand-throwing guidelines—such as “this voice requires a tall, narrow shoulder with a flared lip.” I created a physical template sheet for each participant’s voice to use while throwing the vessel. This retained the digital logic while leaving space for human touch, deviation, and imperfection.

Surface Data: Translating Music

On the surface level, I focused on representing participants’ chosen songs. Using p5.js, I ran FFT (Fast Fourier Transform) analysis on each song’s audio to generate visual data: amplitude, frequency, and beat timing. I used these data points to create linear wave-like forms that I then traced and transferred to the clay vessels post-throwing. Each song’s rhythm and emotional pacing showed up as raised, textural “scores” wrapped around the vessel—like musical notation frozen in clay. Participants later remarked that touching these textures reminded them of specific crescendos or lyrics they loved.

Unexpected Learnings

What I thought would be a limitation—losing the 3D printer—ended up revealing a new possibility. The vessels became less about precision and more about interpretation. There was beauty in reading the data as a human and interpreting it bodily, like a musician reading sheet music. It emphasized the project’s core theme: the translation of invisible emotion into visible, tactile form.

Next Steps

With all the vessels thrown and initial surface textures carved, I’ll now begin the bisque firing stage and continue refining my documentation. In parallel, I’m preparing visualizations that show how the data becomes vessel—combining waveform, human hand, and final artifact into one narrative.
